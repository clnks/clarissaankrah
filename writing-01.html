The Most Photographed People in the World Just Sold You a Camera

Meta's Kylie Jenner glasses are a masterclass in using celebrity to normalise ambient surveillance, and a revealing case study in how Silicon Valley finally figured out who really controls consumer spending.

June 2026 12 min read Meta · Smart Glasses · Ambient Surveillance · Data Protection

Something is perplexing about the product brief that produced the Meta Starfire Kylie Edition. The glasses feature a small gem on the right lens, positioned, according to the product launch materials, to catch light "the way camera flashes catch Kylie." In other words, a piece of hardware explicitly designed around the experience of being relentlessly photographed and surveilled. And it comes fitted with a 12-megapixel camera that will photograph everyone around you, hands-free, all day long.

This is not an accident. It is the point.

Meta's campaign for the Starfire Kylie Edition. Source: Meta.

Last week, Meta dropped the Ray-Ban branding from its smart glasses line and launched three new models under its own name: the Adventurer, the Fury, and the flagship Starfire, co-designed with Kylie Jenner. The Starfire starts at $399, includes a Kylie AI voice option, and is the first major wearable tech collaboration for a family that has, for over fifteen years, made surveillance their business model — just with themselves as the product.

01

Silicon Valley finally figured out who controls the till

The partnership has been accompanied by a comment circulating widely online, to the effect that Silicon Valley has finally worked out who actually controls consumer spending: the Kardashians. It reads as a joke. It shouldn't.

What the Kardashian-Jenner family has built over fifteen years isn't just fame; it's a framework for making people comfortable with visibility. Every product they've created, from shapewear to cosmetics to tequila, lands in front of an audience that already trusts them as a guide to what's worth wanting. That's an enormously valuable thing to attach to a camera. It genuinely shapes how people feel about a product category.

Meta has struggled to crack mainstream consumer adoption of smart glasses beyond the early-adopter tech demographic. Daily users of its glasses tripled year-over-year in 2025, which sounds impressive until you consider that the total numbers remained modest relative to the size of the overall market. The Jenner partnership is a deliberate attempt to reach a different audience entirely: people who would never buy a piece of hardware because of its specs, but who absolutely will because their favourite influencer says it matches her aesthetic.

Context Meta reportedly sold more than 7 million pairs of smart glasses in 2025 — a sharp increase, but still a fraction of the consumer electronics market. The Kylie Edition, priced at $399, targets a demographic already comfortable spending on aspirational lifestyle products.
02

We have been here before — and we forgot

When Google Glass launched in 2013 with enormous fanfare and equally enormous backlash, Glass was essentially killed by the social response it provoked. "Glassholes" became a term of genuine contempt. Public spaces banned them, from bars to cinemas. People became viscerally uncomfortable with a device that could be recording at any moment, worn by someone who looked like they were just standing in a queue.

The technology then was less capable than what Meta is shipping now. But what is important to note is that what is different today is not the hardware; it is the cultural groundwork. A decade of social media has normalised the idea that being in public means being content. Ring doorbells record neighbours. TikTok normalises pointing a camera at strangers. And crucially, the Kardashians have spent fifteen years demonstrating that surveillance is not something that happens to you; it is something you perform in order for relevance and visibility.

And this is where the Kardashian-Jenner family's role becomes genuinely interesting, rather than just ironic. Kylie Jenner has spent her adult life navigating one of the most extreme versions of public visibility imaginable — paparazzi, reality TV, social media scrutiny at a scale most of us can't picture. She's also built a business on understanding exactly how to control the conditions under which she's seen. That expertise, that literacy around image and visibility, is precisely what Meta is buying into. The question worth asking is what it means when that expertise gets pointed outward — at the rest of us.

The most surveilled family in American culture is now a vector for surveilling everyone else. The oxymoron is structural.

03

What the glasses actually collect

From a data protection standpoint, the glasses present a layered set of concerns that go beyond the obvious camera question. Meta updated its privacy policy for the Ray-Ban line in April 2025, making two significant changes:

Policy changes — April 2025
  1. Voice recording storage became mandatory and could not be opted out of.
  2. The AI camera features remain active unless users specifically disable the wake word. The new Meta Glasses line inherits this policy framework.

This means that by default, audio captured by the device is uploaded and stored. Photos and videos captured with the glasses are processed according to Meta's AI and cloud policies when shared to those services. In practice, the chain of data flows from the wearer's face, through Meta's infrastructure, and, as a lawsuit filed earlier this year revealed, potentially to human reviewers at third-party annotation contractors. Swedish investigators reported that workers at a Nairobi-based subcontractor had reviewed private footage captured through customers' glasses, including sensitive content. Meta confirmed the use of human review in its terms of service, but the disclosure was buried and disputed by users who felt they had not meaningfully consented.

I-XRAY — 2024 demonstration Two Harvard students built a system combining Ray-Ban Meta glasses with facial recognition and public database lookups to identify strangers on the street in real time — pulling home addresses, phone numbers, and other personal details from a glance. They chose not to release the code, framing it as a warning. The warning was largely not heeded.

Meta has said it does not include facial recognition in its smart glasses, citing ethical concerns, though reports have suggested the company may revisit this position for future models. But the I-XRAY demonstration was not about Meta's built-in features. It was about what becomes possible when consumer hardware meets the existing data ecosystem. The glasses are the lens. The data is already out there.

04

Legal landscape

Under the Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA), collecting biometric data — including via facial recognition — requires written consent. Several US states have similar provisions. The GDPR in the UK and the EU imposes strict conditions on the processing of biometric data. However, recording in public spaces is generally legal in most jurisdictions, and the legal line between "capturing a face" and "processing biometric data" remains actively contested.

The Information Commissioner's Office opened an investigation into Meta's glasses practices earlier this year, following the subcontractor footage controversy.

05

Zuckerberg's real acquisition

Meta's move away from the Ray-Ban brand is significant. Ray-Ban is, as Meta's own CTO acknowledged, iconic, and that iconicity provided a form of brand insulation. When a company with Meta's privacy track record puts its name front and centre on a camera people wear on their face, it is taking a risk. The bet, clearly, is that cultural endorsement can do what brand heritage used to do.

This is the real nature of what Zuckerberg has acquired. An audience's trust in a person and that person's demonstrated ability to make her followers want what she has. Meta has spent years building platforms that the Kardashians used to become influential. Now it is buying that influence back — as a distribution channel for hardware it cannot sell on its own merits.

The audience does not want to be sold a product by an engineer. It wants to be sold a life. And no one sells a life more effectively than someone whose life has itself been a product from the beginning.

06

What data protection means in a world of ambient capture

The practical challenge for data protection, from a regulatory and governance perspective, is that the current framework was built around a model of deliberate data collection: you fill out a form, you share a document, you sign up for a service. Ambient capture — the continuous, incidental, often invisible recording that wearable cameras enable — breaks that model at the level of design.

Bystanders captured in the background of someone's Kylie glasses footage have not consented to anything. They may not know they have been recorded. Their faces, voices, and surroundings may be used to train AI systems they have never interacted with. This is, I believe, the real catch: data is so valuable that the business case for ambient capture exists entirely separately from the glasses themselves. Existing data protection law, including GDPR, has provisions around lawful basis and purpose limitation, but enforcing those principles against hardware that captures the physical world in real time, across millions of devices, is a fundamentally different regulatory challenge than auditing a database.

07

The consent we never gave

There is a version of this story in which Kylie Jenner's involvement is irrelevant — just a marketing tactic for a piece of hardware that raises the same questions regardless of who designed the case. That version is probably correct, technically. But it misses something.

Influence works by making things feel safe, desirable, and normal. The Kardashians have spent their entire careers doing exactly that for products ranging from cosmetics to cryptocurrency. When someone with 400 million followers normalises wearing a camera on her face, she is not just selling glasses. She is shifting the baseline of what people expect from public space.

Google Glass failed partly because it felt alien. Because the person wearing it looked like they were doing something — recording you. Meta's bet, with the Kylie Edition, is that glasses that look like something you would actually wear will not provoke the same reaction. That the gem will catch the light beautifully. That you will not think about what the camera sees.

From a data protection standpoint, that is precisely the problem. The less a surveillance device looks like surveillance, the less likely people are to exercise whatever limited rights they have in response to it. Consent requires awareness. Awareness requires visibility. And the entire design philosophy of these glasses — fashionable, subtle, aspirational — is oriented toward making the camera disappear.

Silicon Valley may have finally figured out who controls consumer spending. What remains to be worked out is who controls what comes next — and whether the answer to that question will be anyone other than the platforms already holding the data.
