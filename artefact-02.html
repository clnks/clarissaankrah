Fictional Scenario · Case Study · 2026

DPIAAI-Powered CV Screening Tool

Article 35 Automated Decision-Making Special Category Data UK GDPR

The Brief

A thirty-person HR-tech company is introducing an AI tool that scores CVs against a job description, ranks them, and surfaces the top decile for human review. The model uses a third-party LLM with a vendor-supplied fine-tune on historic hiring outcomes. The company processes ~10,000 applications per month across its clients.

The vendor's pitch frames the tool as "decision support". The company's recruiters describe it, accurately, as "the thing that decides who we look at". That gap between marketing language and operational reality is the entire reason a DPIA is mandatory here, not optional.

Framework

Why a DPIA is mandatory

Three Article 35 triggers, any one of which would suffice:

  • Automated decision-making with significant effect (Article 35(3)(a)) The tool's ranking determines who progresses; the bottom 90% are not seen by a human. Calling this "human in the loop" mislabels what is happening: humans are downstream of the decision that matters most.
  • Special category data, inferred (Article 35(3)(b)) CVs reveal religion, health, trade-union activity, and often ethnicity by proxy. The model does not need to be told these things to learn them.
  • Large-scale processing (Article 35(3)(b)) 10,000 applications per month across multiple clients meets any reasonable reading of "large scale".

The ICO's own list of operations requiring a DPIA also names "innovative technology" and "use of AI" as triggers. This scenario fires on every relevant threshold.

Analysis

Risk register

The full register identified twelve risks. The five highest-priority entries:

Risk Likelihood Severity Mitigation
Disparate impact on protected groups via proxy variables in the fine-tuning data. High High Pre-deployment bias audit by independent third party; quarterly demographic outcome review; rollback plan if disparate impact exceeds 4/5ths rule.
Lack of meaningful information about the logic involved (Article 13(2)(f)). Certain Medium Plain-language explanation in candidate-facing notice; per-decision factor list available on request via DSAR-equivalent route.
No genuine human review for rejected candidates. High High Mandatory human sampling of bottom-decile rejections (5% sample); review by recruiter not involved in original sift; documented override authority.
Special category data inferred and processed without explicit basis. Medium High Configure model to ignore named demographic fields; statistical test for inferred categories; suppression of any feature with measurable demographic loading.
Vendor (third country) processing of CV content via LLM API. Certain Medium UK IDTA in place; data residency in UK/EU regions; no training on customer data contractually warranted; processor audit annually.

Consultation: Article 35(9) requires the views of data subjects to be sought where appropriate. We ran a structured consultation with twelve recent applicants across three client industries; their feedback fed directly into the candidate-facing transparency notice and the human-review sampling protocol.

Residual risk assessment

After applying the mitigations above, residual risk is assessed as medium. The dominant residual risk is disparate impact that emerges in deployment but does not appear in pre-deployment testing. Model behaviour on a live distribution of applicants can diverge from the audit set. This is monitored quarterly with an agreed rollback trigger.

No prior consultation with the ICO under Article 36 is required: residual risk is not "high" after mitigation. This judgement is documented and signed off by the DPO, and the DPIA will be re-run on any material change to the model, the training data, or the client base.

What this taught me

A DPIA is not a form. It is a forcing function for honesty. The most useful conversation in this exercise was not about Article 35. It was about the recruiters describing how they actually used the tool, which contradicted the vendor's framing. Without that conversation, the document would have documented a different product than the one being deployed.

I will carry forward a habit: describe the system as the people operating it describe it, not as the people selling it describe it. The legal analysis flows correctly from the second description and incorrectly from the first.
